Crop Ecology: Plant Interference

Nov 27, 2026 · @Lawrence

Crop Ecology is the study of how crops interact with their physical environment and with other plants, and it underlies almost every practical decision a farmer or agronomist makes: which crop to grow in which zone, how close together to plant it, when to weed, and how to combine crops in the same field. Plant Interference — the subject of this note — is the natural starting point for the course, because before the ecological zones, crop distribution patterns and ecosystem processes covered later can make sense, students need a working understanding of how individual plants affect one another when they grow close together.

In Nigeria, these interactions are not abstract biology; they are the daily reality of the farming systems that feed the country. Mixed cropping and intercropping — maize with cassava, cocoa under plantain, cowpea between cereal rows — rely on predictable patterns of facilitation and competition between the component crops. Crop–weed competition is the single most pervasive constraint on smallholder yields across the country's ecological zones, and it is compounded in the savanna cereal and legume belts by the parasitic weed Striga, which depends entirely on host crops to complete its life cycle and can cause total crop failure under heavy infestation. A sound grasp of plant interference therefore gives students the conceptual tools to explain why a cropping system succeeds or fails, and to recommend practical responses — timing, spacing, cultivar choice and cropping pattern — grounded in ecological reasoning rather than custom alone.

Learning Objectives and Key Definitions

By the end of this topic, students should be able to:

  • Define plant interference and distinguish an ecological community from a population.
  • Distinguish intra-specific from inter-specific plant interactions, with examples from Nigerian cropping systems.
  • Classify any plant-to-plant effect as positive, negative or neutral, and name the three negative mechanisms.
  • Explain competition, amensalism and parasitism, and state what separates each from the others.
  • Apply these concepts to practical decisions in weed management, cultivar choice and cropping-system design.

Plant interference is the full range of interactions — positive, negative and neutral — that occur among individual plants growing close enough to affect one another's access to resources or growing conditions.

An ecological community is an assemblage of populations of two or more different species that interact, directly or indirectly, within a defined area. A single crop stand of one species growing in isolation is a population, not a community; a community exists the moment a second species — a companion crop, a weed, a pest, or a soil organism — shares that space.

Interactions within a community are either intra-specific, occurring between individuals of the same species (for example, over-crowded maize plants competing with one another), or inter-specific, occurring between two or more different species (for example, a cowpea crop and a grass weed, or cocoa growing under a plantain canopy). The same pair of plants can be in an intra-specific relationship in one context and part of an inter-specific one in another — what matters is which two individuals are being compared.

Positive, Negative and Neutral Interactions

Every plant-to-plant interaction can be classified by its net effect on each participant:

  • Positive (facilitation): one plant improves the establishment, growth or survival of a neighbour, usually by moderating the microclimate or improving resource availability.
  • Negative: one plant reduces the fitness of another, through competition, amensalism or parasitism (each defined below).
  • Neutral: two plants coexist with no detectable effect on one another.

Facilitation is well documented in both natural and agricultural plant communities (Bertness & Callaway, 1994; Callaway & Walker, 1997). A nurse plant facilitates an establishing neighbour by casting shade, buffering wind, moderating temperature and humidity, or conserving soil moisture. The classic Nigerian example is the use of plantain (Musa spp.) as a temporary shade crop for young cocoa (Theobroma cacao): field trials at the Cocoa Research Institute of Nigeria and at the Federal University of Technology, Akure, have shown that moderate plantain shade lowers soil and air temperature and improves microclimatic conditions during the vulnerable establishment phase, while excessive shade can depress later flowering and yield — so the benefit is real but depends on managing shade intensity rather than assuming more shade is always better (Agele, Famuwagun & Ogunleye, 2016). Many cocoa farmers in south-eastern Nigeria use banana as a close substitute for plantain for the same purpose (Agbongiarhuoyi et al., 2016).

Facilitation is not unconditional: as the nurse and the facilitated plant both grow, their relationship can shift toward competition, and the balance between the two depends on the developmental stage of each plant and on environmental stress — facilitation tends to be most valuable for a crop under harsh conditions (poor soil, intense sun, water stress) and least valuable once the crop is well established (Callaway & Walker, 1997). Students should therefore avoid presenting any single nurse-crop arrangement as universally beneficial; its value is stage- and context-dependent.

Competition for Resources

Competition is a mutually negative interaction: both plants compete for the same limited resource — light, water, nutrients or space — and each reduces the amount available to the other, so both are harmed to some degree. This is the feature that separates competition from amensalism, discussed in the next section.

Intra-specific competition occurs among individuals of the same species, typically from over-crowding, and is managed agronomically through thinning, pruning and correct planting density. Inter-specific competition in a crop field takes two forms: crop–crop competition between two cultivated species sharing a field (as in mixed cropping), and crop–weed competition, which occurs in both mono-cropped and mixed systems and is usually the larger constraint on yield.

Symmetric and asymmetric competition. Competition for soil nutrients, immediately after germination, tends to be roughly symmetric: each plant is affected in proportion to its own size. Competition for light is usually asymmetric: it only begins once one plant is tall enough to shade another, and the taller or earlier-established plant then captures a disproportionate share of light at the smaller plant's expense (Weiner, 1990, cited in Vermeulen et al., 2016). Relative emergence time is therefore one of the strongest predictors of a competitive outcome: a crop that emerges before a flush of weeds gains an early asymmetric advantage, while a crop that emerges after an existing weed cohort is placed at a disadvantage from the outset (Swanton, Nkoa & Blackshaw, 2015). This is the biological basis for the agronomic concept of a critical period for weed control — the window during early crop growth within which weeds must be removed to prevent yield loss (Labrada, 2003).

Weed suppression versus crop tolerance. These two traits of a crop cultivar are often confused but are distinct. Weed-suppressive ability is the cultivar's capacity to reduce a weed's growth, seed production or establishment — what the crop does to the weed. Tolerance is the cultivar's capacity to maintain its own yield despite weed pressure — what happens to the crop. A review of cereal cultivars found that a cultivar can be strongly suppressive without being especially tolerant, or vice versa, and that the relationship between the two traits is not yet fully predictable from a single plant characteristic (Andrew, Storkey & Sparkes, 2015). In Nigerian systems, bushy cassava cultivars and cover crops such as sweet potato and melon are commonly cited for combining good weed suppression with reasonable tolerance (cf. evaluations of cassava-variety weed tolerance in West Africa). The underlying traits include early vigour, growth rate, biomass accumulation, leaf area and leaf angle, planting density, and — in cereals — tillering capacity.

Amensalism and Allelopathy

Amensalism is an interaction in which one plant is harmed and the other is unaffected. This is the key distinction from competition: in competition both plants draw down a shared resource and both are harmed; in amensalism only one plant suffers a cost, while the other neither gains nor loses.

Allelopathy is the best-studied mechanism of amensalism in plants: one plant releases biochemicals (allelochemicals) into the environment — through root exudation, leaching from leaves, volatilisation, or decomposition of plant residues — that affect a neighbouring plant's germination, growth or survival. Allelochemicals can act directly on the target plant, or indirectly, where soil microorganisms first transform the exuded compound into a different, often more biologically active, form before it affects the neighbour; indirect effects also include allelochemical suppression of nodulation in legume crops.

Two points of scientific nuance matter here. First, the mechanism by which an allelochemical reaches a target plant can be direct, but whether that chemical accumulates to a biologically active concentration, and how strongly it affects the target, still depends heavily on environmental conditions — allelochemical production itself is often induced or amplified by environmental stress on the producing plant, and its persistence in soil depends on temperature, moisture and microbial breakdown (Labrada, 2003). So even a "direct" allelopathic effect is not independent of the surrounding environment; what the direct/indirect distinction separates is the chemical pathway (target plant alone, versus mediation by a third organism), not whether environmental conditions matter. Allelopathic potency is also shaped by the genotype and growth stage of the producing plant and by the extent of microbial degradation of the compound in the soil rhizosphere (Cheng & Cheng, 2015).

Second, allelopathy and amensalism are not simply interchangeable terms. Since Rice's (1984) foundational definition, allelopathy has been understood to include both inhibitory and stimulatory effects of one plant's chemicals on another. When the effect is inhibitory, the interaction is indeed a form of amensalism (one plant harmed, the other unaffected by the act of producing the chemical). But when an allelochemical stimulates a neighbour's germination or growth — as some host-root exudates do for the parasitic weed Striga, whose seeds use host chemical signals as a germination cue — the producing plant is not unaffected by that stimulation (it is typically harmed by the organism it has unwittingly triggered), and the interaction no longer fits the strict definition of amensalism at all. Allelopathy is therefore better understood as a chemical mechanism that can underlie several different interaction outcomes, of which amensalism is only the inhibitory case, not as a synonym for amensalism.

Parasitism

Parasitism is always harmful to the host plant. Parasitic plants penetrate host tissue with a specialised organ, the haustorium, which connects to the host's vascular system and allows the parasite to withdraw water and nutrients; depending on the species, a haustorial connection can also move small molecules such as RNA, proteins and plant hormones between host and parasite (Kokla et al., 2022). Parasitic plants are not a single uniform group, and how dependent each is on its host varies considerably.

Holoparasites (for example dodders and broomrapes) lack chlorophyll entirely and cannot photosynthesise, so they depend completely on the host for both water and organic nutrients.

Hemiparasites retain chlorophyll and photosynthesise at least part of their own food, so they are only partially dependent on the host. Mistletoes (Loranthaceae and Viscaceae, including the Tapinanthus species common on Nigerian trees) are obligate stem hemiparasites: they photosynthesise but still require a host connection, and their haustoria draw mainly water and dissolved minerals from the host's xylem. In Nigeria, mistletoe infestation is a recognised problem on tree crops including cocoa, kolanut and citrus, where heavy infestation can reduce host vigour, though the severity depends on infestation load and host condition rather than being uniformly destructive (Krasylenko et al., 2022).

Striga (witchweed) is a different case again: despite having green, chlorophyll-bearing leaves, it is an obligate root hemiparasite of the family Orobanchaceae — it cannot complete its life cycle without a host. Its seeds remain dormant in the soil until they detect strigolactones, chemical signals exuded by the roots of a suitable host; germination and attachment are tightly synchronised with the host's own root development. Once attached, Striga forms a xylem bridge through which it withdraws water and nutrients from the host, and documented effects of Striga and related root parasites extend to altering host hormone levels, not merely withdrawing resources (Runo & Kuria, 2018). Striga species differ in host range — S. hermonthica and S. asiatica are the major constraints on cereals such as sorghum, maize, millet and upland rice across Nigeria's savanna zones, while S. gesnerioides specialises on cowpea — so control measures (resistant varieties, trap and intercrops, timely weeding, soil fertility management) must be matched to the species and host crop in question rather than applied as a single generic remedy.

Practical Implications for Crop Establishment and Management

Understanding plant interference translates into a short list of agronomic responses, each of which should be applied with judgement rather than as a universal rule:

  • Match planting density to the species, using thinning and pruning to limit intra-specific competition within a stand; the correct density varies by crop, cultivar and soil fertility.
  • Protect the crop's emergence advantage by planting into a clean seedbed and controlling the first flush of weeds within the critical period, since an early-emerging crop gains a lasting asymmetric advantage over later-emerging weeds — but the length of that critical period itself varies with weed pressure and growing conditions, so it should be determined locally rather than assumed.
  • Select cultivars for weed suppression or tolerance — for example bushy cassava types or cover crops such as sweet potato and melon — while recognising that a cultivar strong in one trait is not automatically strong in the other, and that yield under weed-free conditions can trade off against competitive ability.
  • Scout for parasitic weeds and hemiparasites early, since both Striga and mistletoe are far easier to manage before an infestation is established; appropriate responses (resistant or tolerant varieties, trap cropping, rogueing, fertility management) should match the specific parasite and host rather than being applied generically.
  • Use facilitation deliberately but attentively, as with plantain or banana shade for young cocoa: moderate shade can aid establishment, but shade intensity needs active management, since excessive shade can depress later flowering and yield.

None of these responses is a guarantee of success in isolation; plant interference operates alongside soil, climate, pest and market factors, and recommendations should always be checked against local conditions rather than presented as universally effective.

Summary, Glossary and Discussion Questions

Summary

Plant interference covers every interaction that occurs among plants growing close together in an ecological community, whether between individuals of the same species (intra-specific) or different species (inter-specific). Each interaction is positive (facilitation), negative (competition, amensalism or parasitism), or neutral. Competition harms both participants as they draw on a shared limited resource; amensalism harms only one, most often through allelopathy, a chemical mechanism whose effects can be inhibitory or stimulatory and whose intensity depends on genotype, environment, growth stage and soil microbial activity; and parasitism harms the host outright, through a spectrum running from fully dependent holoparasites to partially dependent hemiparasites such as mistletoe and the deceptively green but still obligate parasite Striga. These concepts give Nigerian crop producers a basis for decisions on planting density, weed-control timing, cultivar choice and cropping system design.

Glossary

  • Plant interference — the range of interactions among plants growing in close proximity.
  • Ecological community — an assemblage of two or more interacting species sharing a defined area.
  • Intra-specific / inter-specific interaction — interaction between individuals of the same species / of different species.
  • Facilitation — a positive interaction in which one plant improves conditions for another.
  • Nurse plant — a plant whose presence facilitates the establishment of another.
  • Competition — a mutually negative interaction over a shared limited resource.
  • Symmetric / asymmetric competition — competitive effects proportional to plant size / disproportionately favouring the larger or earlier plant.
  • Amensalism — an interaction in which one plant is harmed and the other is unaffected.
  • Allelopathy — a chemical mechanism by which one plant's exudates affect another, with inhibitory or stimulatory effects.
  • Haustorium — the specialised organ by which a parasitic plant connects to a host's vascular system.
  • Holoparasite / hemiparasite — a parasite wholly dependent on its host for nutrition / a parasite that still photosynthesises.

Discussion Questions

  • Explain the plant interactions that may occur in a mixed cropping or intercropping system, naming at least one positive and one negative interaction.
  • If a crop exhibits allelopathy, what negative and positive effects could this have in crop production, and why can the same trait cut both ways?
  • Name two cropping systems in which a nurse plant is useful, and explain the role the nurse plant plays in each.
  • Why is it inaccurate to describe Striga simply as a "green plant" that is not really parasitic? What evidence would you use to correct this view?
  • A farmer wants to use a crop's known allelopathic ability as their main weed-control strategy. What would you want to know about the crop, the weed and the field before advising them?
